CLIMB · experiment 1 · uniform vs error-adaptive clip sampling

Sampler Collapse

The error-adaptive sampler loses to uniform on every seed — and the telemetry shows why: it concentrates 88% of all sampling mass onto a single clip out of a hundred.

2026-08-16RTX 5090 · sm_120 mjlab v1.6.0 + climb extension6 runs · 54 evaluations

01 The question

fable.md H2 claims a grounded adaptive sampler buys a 2–3× compute reduction over uniform and error-adaptive baselines. Before testing the grounded sampler, the comparator itself has to be established: does error-adaptive sampling beat uniform at all?

This matters because the prior SONIC track-B program answered "no" once already — SIM-M3 recorded adaptive as worse than uniform (ΔMPJPE-G = +0.111, 1/3 seeds improved, permutation p = 0.875) — but on a motion bank that failed its own dataset headroom gate, which makes that null uninterpretable. The experiment here is the same question asked on a bank that passes the gate.

02 Method

What was built first

mjlab's tracking task loads exactly one motion clip. Its adaptive mode samples the start frame within that clip by failure weight; there is no clip axis. So the comparison needed a multi-clip capability before it could be run at all.

MotionBank concatenates every clip along the time axis and keeps a per-clip offset table, making time_steps a global index. That leaves all ~20 of mjlab's reference accessors working untouched, so the only thing that changes between this and stock mjlab is which clip an environment is on.

Four choices that decide whether the answer means anything

  1. Uniform over clips, not frames. The bank spans 3.7 s to 264 s. Sampling frames uniformly would weight by duration and quietly turn the control arm into a length-weighted curriculum, confounding the comparison it exists to anchor.
  2. Held-out and difficulty-matched. Generalisation needs disjoint eval clips — but drawing them uniformly from the clean bank would make the eval set far easier than a training bank that is half hardest-decile, and every arm would saturate.
  3. Curves, not endpoints. "Compute reduction" is a claim about performance per unit compute. Reaching the same score sooner and reaching a higher score are different claims and only a curve separates them. This turned out to matter: the gap at iteration 2500 is 5× the gap at 4000.
  4. Indexed by iteration, not wall-clock. The GPU is shared. Wall-clock would absorb contention from unrelated jobs and report it as a sampler effect.

The mechanism: the sampler collapses

A single clip out of one hundred absorbs 87–89% of all sampling mass, in every seed. Entropy bottoms between iterations 1500 and 2500 — exactly where the performance gap peaks at 0.145 — and the gap narrows as entropy partially recovers. That is not a correlation read off two noisy series; it is the same timeline in both.

Why the "10% uniform floor" did not prevent it

Both mjlab's formulation and this clip-level port combine the uniform prior additively:

p = failure_ema + uniform_ratio / N     # then normalise

failure_ema holds failure counts, not a normalised distribution. So the uniform term's share of total mass is ε / (Σq + ε). With ε = 0.1 and Σq in the tens — routine at 4096 environments — uniform retains well under 1% of the mass, not the 10% its name implies.

adaptive_uniform_ratio is not a floor. Its effective strength varies with the failure rate and with num_envs: tune it at 1024 environments and it behaves differently at 4096. This is inherited from mjlab, so it applies to the upstream bin-level sampler too, not only to this port.

Normalising q before mixing makes ε scale-invariant. That one change is the grounded arm, and it holds coverage: top-1 mass peaks at 0.186 and decays to 0.045, entropy never drops below 0.754 and rises to 0.983.

04 What this shows, and what it does not

Shows. On a bank that passes the headroom gate, error-adaptive clip sampling is reliably worse than uniform — 3/3 seeds, ~20 seed s.d., and worse in AULC (0.640 vs 0.698) by more than twice its endpoint deficit. The cause is measured, not inferred: the sampler collapses onto one clip, and the collapse timeline matches the performance timeline.

Does not show that adaptive curricula fail in general. The arm tested is the comparator — mjlab's failure-weighted form — not CLIMB's proposed grounded sampler. If anything the result strengthens the case for grounding: the failure mode fable.md §3.4 invokes to motivate the grounding term has now been observed directly, with a magnitude and a timeline.

Other limits worth stating plainly: 4000 of 30,000 iterations, so this is the rising portion of the curve rather than converged performance; one bank of 100 clips; one robot; one reward configuration; survival rate as the outcome, not tracking accuracy. The early crossover — adaptive genuinely leads by 0.043 at iteration 500 — suggests the failure is over-concentration rather than the failure signal being useless, but three seeds at one checkpoint is thin evidence for that reading.

05 Current goal and open questions

The immediate goal has shifted from "measure the curriculum effect" to "establish whether grounding is what makes a curriculum work" — because the comparator failed in a specific, diagnosable way rather than a diffuse one.

Running now

Three grounded seeds on the same bank, same budget, same held-out set. Three outcomes and what each would mean:

If grounded…Then
beats uniformthe strongest possible result — grounding converts a harmful curriculum into a helpful one, and it is the mechanism, not the prioritisation, that matters
matches uniformgrounding repairs the damage but prioritisation adds nothing at this scale; the honest paper is about why curricula fail, and the bank-diversity axis becomes the make-or-break test
still losescollapse was not the cause; something about clip-level failure weighting is itself harmful, and the frontier premise needs rethinking

Open questions, in priority order

  1. Is the frontier premise even satisfied here? Per-clip survival is close to bimodal — the policy tends to master a clip or fail it. A frontier sampler assumes a population of clips near p(success) ≈ 0.5 to concentrate on. Measuring how populated that band actually is, and how it moves during training, may explain the prior program's ZPD null better than any sampler comparison.
  2. Does the curriculum × diversity interaction exist? The plan's bet is that curricula pay off as bank diversity grows. This ran at 100 clips; 800 is available and screened. If the effect is absent at 100 and present at 800, that is the paper. If it is absent at both, that is also a result — and a cheaper one to establish than the full matrix.
  3. What is the right unit of prioritisation? Clip-level weighting collapsed. mjlab's segment-level weighting within a clip did not, in its single-clip setting. That difference is testable and would say whether the collapse is about granularity rather than about adaptivity.
  4. Does the difficulty atlas transfer across policies? Offline physics features predict this policy's difficulty out-of-fold at ρ ≈ 0.74. If the same features predict a differently-trained policy's difficulty, the atlas is a property of the motions; if not, it is a property of the run, and its use for bank construction is much weaker.
  5. Is survival the right outcome? It is coarse and it saturates. Tracking error is graded but was compressed (1.48× spread) on the easy bank. A blended outcome may resolve differences the current one cannot.

What changed in the plan as a result

Three concrete revisions, all evidence-backed rather than editorial:

  • Grounding becomes the primary contrast, not an ablation. It is the only genuinely novel component, and the term it is bolted onto now has a measured failure mode.
  • Every bank must pass the headroom gate before it enters the matrix. An evenly-stratified 50-clip bank left the policy mastering 88% of clips after 13% of a run — a sampler comparison there returns an uninterpretable null, which is what SIM-M3 was.
  • Report magnitude-aware statistics alongside the sign test. At the measured seed reproducibility (±0.005), the sign test's p-floor, not the data, is the binding constraint on significance.
